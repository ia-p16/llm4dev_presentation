## Slide 2 — Why this is a problem for maintainers 
<v-clicks>

- AI lowers the *cost of contributing* 📉
- But it raises the *cost of maintaining* 📈
- Maintainers are usually volunteers with limited time⌛
- The result: a "maintenance tax" paid by a few, generated by many.

</v-clicks>

<v-click>

| Hassle | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| Volume | More PRs, LoCs and issues than ever, many low-quality |
| Verification cost | Maintainers must check correctness, security, style, relevance|
| Authorship blur | Hard to know who (or what) wrote a change |
| Hallucinated reports | Bug reports / security claims that don't reproduce |
| Review fatigue | More work per submission, higher burnout risk |
| Trust erosion | "Is this person even reading the code?" |

</v-click>

> "AI eliminates the natural effort-based backpressure that previously limited low-quality submissions." [^11]

[^11]: [You can contribute if you… An Empirical Framework of AI Contribution Policies in OSS (arXiv, 2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07919v1)

## Slide 4 — Case study: curl
**The bug bounty that broke under AI slop**
<img v-drag="'curl'" src="/images/curl-30-years.svg">

- Early 2024: Daniel Stenberg(curl,curllib Founder) starts complaining about AI-generated bug reports.
- 2025: ~20% of curl bug bounty submissions are **AI slop**; only ~5% are real vulnerabilities.
- May 2025: curl adds a checkbox requiring disclosure of AI use.
- July 2025: Stenberg publicly considers killing the bug bounty.[^1]
- January 2026: curl shuts down the bug bounty program entirely.[^2]

N.B. curl publishes submitted AI slop security reports [^222]
### AI policy: 
    - It is always the contributor reponsibility to respect the license / ownership
    - Security report: understand it, reproduce it, and write it in your own language
    - PR: follow the standards, be ready for questions if Spotted
    - translations: make it looks like human talk
    
**Lesson:** Even with disclosure, low-quality AI reports can overwhelm a small maintainer team.

[^1]: [curl vs AI: Daniel Stenberg interview (2025)](https://opensourcesecurity.io/2025/2025-05-curl_vs_ai_with_daniel_stenberg/)

[^2]: [curl: Shutting down the bug bounty due to AI slop (2026)](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/01/21/curl-shutters-bug-bounty-program-to-stop-ai-slop/5063039)

[^222]: [AI slop security reports submitted to curl](https://gist.github.com/bagder/07f7581f6e3d78ef37dfbfc81fd1d1cd)

## Slide 5 — Case study: Linux kernel
<img v-drag="'square'" src="/images/linux_kernel.png">

**From debate to formal policy (2025–2026)**
- Late 2025: Maintainers Summit pushes for a documented consensus.
- April 2026: Kernel adopts a formal AI coding assistants policy.
- Oct 2026: Removed "Assisted-by" tag from the documentation [^3]
- Key rules as of 2026-11-05 [^5]:
  - AI-assisted contributions are allowed.
  - AI agents cannot add the legally binding `Signed-off-by` tag.
  - Humans should add their own Signed-off-by tag to certify the DCO (Developer Ceritificate of Ownership)
  - Full legal and technical accountability stays with the human submitter.
  - Patches must still comply with GPL-2.0-only, respect licensing rules.

>Linus Trovalds:"AI is a tool, just like other tools we use.  And it's clearly a useful one.
It may not have been that \"clearly\" even just a year ago, but it's no longer in question today."[^4]

[^4]: [Linux kernel thread July 14-2026 - L.Trovalds](https://lore.kernel.org/linux-media/CAHk-=wi4zC+Ze8e+p3tMv8TtG_80KzsZ1syL9anBtmEh5Z40vg@mail.gmail.com/) 

[^3]: [Commit 1e03529 - docs: remove Assisted-by references](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/1e035297ffdadf2184c3c773b8c098e372324aaf)
[^5]: [Linux kernel: AI Coding Assistants policy (official docs)- last 7.4.0-rc1](https://docs.kernel.org/process/coding-assistants.html)

## Slide 6 — Case study: QEMU
<img v-drag="'square'" src="/images/Qemu.png">

**From blanket ban to nuanced policy attempt**
- First created by Fabrice Bellard in 2003 (Embedded Emulator)
- Original policy: reject any contribution known or suspected to include AI-generated content. (still official):
> "Current QEMU project policy is to DECLINE any contributions which are believed to include or derive from AI generated content. This includes ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Llama and similar tools.
> This policy does not apply to other uses of AI, such as researching APIs or algorithms, static analysis, or debugging, provided their output is not included in contributions."[^8]

- 2026 proposal: relax to allow limited AI assistance.[^7]
- Proposed rules:
  - AI allowed for mechanical changes, tests, docs, small bug fixes (≤20 lines).
  - Larger changes or core code require prior maintainer agreement.
  - New `AI-used-for:` trailer in commit messages.
  - DCO and `Signed-off-by` still required; human stays accountable.[^8]

N.B. QEMU doest not use the usual git flow for submitting contributions[^6]
[^6]: [Qemu docs - Submitting a patch](https://www.qemu.org/docs/master/devel/submitting-a-patch.html#submitting-your-patches)
[^7]: [QEMU: Proposed relaxation of AI contribution policy (2026)](https://lists.nongnu.org/archive/html/qemu-devel/2026-05/msg07614.html)
[^8]: [QEMU: AI Policy Latest](https://www.qemu.org/docs/master/devel/code-provenance.html#use-of-ai-generated-content)

## Slide 7 — Case study: LLVM
<img v-drag="'square'" src="/images/llvm.png">

**Human-in-the-loop, not a ban**
LLVM: A Compilation Framework for Lifelong Program Analysis & Transformation [^10]
The LLVM Project is a collection of modular and reusable compiler and toolchain technologies.
- LLVM does not ban AI-generated contributions.[^9]
- It requires:
  - Authors to stand behind the quality of their code.
  - Disclosure if AI was used to generate the code.
- Focus is on accountability, not prohibition.

> [!NOTE]
> A middle path: allow AI, but make the human own the result.

[^9]: [LLVM AI usage policy](https://llvm.org/docs/AIToolPolicy.html)
[^10]: "LLVM: A Compilation Framework for Lifelong Program Analysis & Transformation", Chris Lattner and Vikram Adve. Proceedings of the 2004 International Symposium on Code Generation and Optimization (CGO'04), Palo Alto, California, Mar. 2004.

## Slide 10 — Red Hat's 3 Pillars framework for AI-assisted development - 7 principles **Policy** 1/2

1. Policy: 
    1. A. For developers
        1. **Human accountability:** The developer submitting AI-generated code owns the security and quality outcomes. AI doesn't sign commits, developers do. 
        2. **Engineering best practices:** Code review, testing, CI/CD gates, and security scans don't disappear because AI wrote the code.
        3. **Security-conscious:** AI-generated code should be treated as untrusted input until validated.
        4. **Security-focused prompts:** Developers must learn to prompt AI assistants toward security-focused patterns. Before submitting code, ask your AI assistant to identify security issues, edge cases, or logic errors

## Slide 11 — Red Hat's 3 Pillars framework for AI-assisted development- 7 principles **Policy** 2/2

## Slide 12 — Red Hat's 3 Pillars framework for AI-assisted development: **Skills** 
2. Skills: Security-focused use of AI

What are security skills?

Security skills encode security best practices, security-focused architecture guidance, software supply chain hardening, and vulnerability testing strategies in a format AI assistants can read and apply. Instead of relying on developers to remember every secure coding principle, skills embed expertise directly into the AI's workflow.[^12]

## Slide 15 — A minimal policy for contributors 
<div v-click>AI-assisted contributions are welcome under these conditions:</div>
<div v-click>1. Disclose material AI use in the PR description.</div>
<div v-click>2. You are responsible for every line, as if you wrote it yourself.</div>
<div v-click>3. You must be able to explain any line or design decision on request.</div>
<div v-click>4. Run and report the project's tests/checks yourself.</div>
<div v-click>5. Keep changes small, focused, and tied to an approved issue.</div>
<div v-click>6. Do not submit fully automated PRs or issues; a human must review before opening.</div>
<div v-click>7. Maintainers may close PRs that violate these rules without further review.</div>

## Slide 16 — Key takeaways

<v-clicks>

- AI is not going away; the question is how to govern it, not whether to allow it.
- The biggest cost is not the code itself, but the *review and trust* burden.
- Clear, lightweight & applicable policies protect both maintainers and genuine contributors.
- Disclosure + human accountability + limited PRs = a sustainable balance.
- Automation (CI/CD, policy-as-code) is becoming a key lever to reduce maintainer burden.

</v-clicks>

## Slide 12 — What good policies look like
Inspired from Fedora post 

1. **Disclosure** – state when AI was used for a material part of the change.
2. **Human responsibility** – the contributor owns every line, as if they wrote it.
3. **Understanding** – be able to explain any line or design decision on request.
4. **Testing evidence** – run and report the project's checks yourself.
5. **Scope limits** – small, focused diffs tied to an approved issue.
6. **No fully automated submissions** – a human must review before opening a PR.
7. **Maintainer discretion** – close policy-violating PRs without further review.

## Slide 14 — Practical workflow for maintainers
 
1. **Triage fast** – reject obvious failures (missing issue link, no tests, huge diff).
2. **Check disclosure** – is AI use declared?
3. **Spot-check understanding** – ask one targeted question about the change.
4. **Verify tests** – do CI checks pass and are they meaningful?
5. **Escalate or close** – good ideas get repaired/coached; bad ones get closed with a pointer to the policy.
